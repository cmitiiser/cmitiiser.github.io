Can Paper Folds Compute?

SATURDAY, AUGUST 29, 2026

In this joint session, the Club of Mathematics (CMIT) and the Coding Club (CCIT) explored the mathematics of origami to investigate whether a physical sheet of paper can compute. Moving across Euclidean geometry, graph theory, and cellular automata, the session concluded with a hands-on workshop constructing functional fold-based logic gates.

The session was framed to show that computation is substrate-independent, proving that a rule as simple as an iterated crease assignment is sufficient to achieve universality.

Session Modules

The Geometry of Folding

Duration: 20 min | Track: Mathematics (CMIT)

Folds were framed as isometric reflections about crease lines, governed by the seven Huzita-Justin axioms. Because fold operations can determine the roots of general cubic polynomials, origami is strictly more powerful than straightedge-and-compass constructions, enabling classical impossibilities such as angle trisection and doubling the cube.

Key Concepts:

  1. Kawasaki's Theorem: Single-vertex flat-foldability via alternating angle sum cancellation.
  2. Maekawa's Theorem: Parity constraints showing mountain and valley fold counts differ by exactly two.
  3. Computational Complexity: Single-vertex tractability contrasted against the NP-hardness of general multi-vertex flat-foldability.

Creases as Networks

Duration: 25 min | Track: Graph Theory & Storage (CCIT)

Crease patterns naturally define graphs where creases represent edges and fold intersections act as vertices. The session analyzed the algorithmic efficiency of how machines represent spatial fold networks.

Key Concepts:

  1. Adjacency matrices (Θ(N²) storage) versus adjacency lists (Θ(N + E) storage) for sparse crease networks.
  2. Network balance and degree distributions analyzed through the Handshake Lemma.
  3. Path traversals and Breadth-First Search (BFS) for algorithmic route verification.

Universality & Rule 110

Duration: 25 min | Track: Computation Theory (CCIT)

Focusing on elementary cellular automata, the session unpacked how computational universality arises from simple, localized transition rules rather than complex hardware.

Key Concepts:

  1. Manual tracing of Rule 110 neighborhood transitions across binary strings.
  2. Information encoding: Localized traveling structures ("gliders") acting as data.
  3. Glider collisions functioning as physical logic gates, proving weak Turing completeness.

The Folding Computer (Hands-on Workshop)

Duration: 30 min | Track: Collaborative Workshop

Participants translated discrete states into physical sheets of paper, utilizing mountain and valley folds to represent binary values (0 and 1). By linking physical fold gadgets sequentially, participants physically executed logical state transitions.

Activity Outline:

  1. Folding a single-inversion NOT gadget.
  2. Extending folded mechanisms to construct composite AND and XOR logic gates.
  3. The Gate-Sequence Challenge: Verifying gate arrangements against truth tables in paired speed trials.
